Abstract

Autoregressive music generation models produce convincing clips but offer composers no way to revise one: a causal decoder can only continue a prompt, never regenerate an interior window while honoring what comes after it, and every such model is bounded to a context of thirty seconds, far short of a finished piece. While several methods proposed to extend the generation up to a 3 min clip, it cannot able to regenerate only a part of the sample.

This report casts editing as infilling --- sampling an interior window conditioned on both its prefix and its suffix --- and reaches it with a single architectural change: an asymmetric span attention mask that lets the edit window attend to the entire context, past and future, while every other position keeps its ordinary causal view. Combined with an edit-mode training recipe, this turns a causal musicgen-style model into a native infiller without touching sampling, guidance, or decoding, and reduces exactly to the original causal model as a special case. On top of this mechanism we build a composer's control layer: a cosine context schedule dialing how strongly the edit adheres to each side of its context, melody conditioning via a chromagram of the edit window, and a dataset-token control routed through adaptive layer normalization.

The same mask, composed with a stream selection over musicgen-stem's per-instrument codebooks, further yields a stem-by-time editor that regenerates one chosen instrument inside one chosen window, and chaining that editor's own continuation mode with its own infilling pass reaches compositions longer than any single context window --- this internship's culminating goal. Every model is trained from scratch on two 48\,GB GPUs, on small, deliberately mismatched corpora, and evaluated against causal and public-checkpoint baselines.

Dataset steering — Schaeffer token (AdaLN)

Forcing the schaeffer dataset token — routed through adaptive layer normalisation (AdaLN) — steers the model toward a tiny target dataset (here called schaeffer). The point is that it adopts that dataset's character without overfitting its few examples, keeping the diversity learned from the large corpus. To make that checkable, each output is shown next to a reference drawn from the tiny dataset — the generations clearly differ from the references rather than reproducing them.

The stem composer in every edit mode

The same model, driven through its four modes. Every clip is 10 seconds at a frame rate of 50, so an edit window given in frames divides by 50 to give seconds — window 123–500 means the model wrote everything from 2.46 s to the end. All of these are unprompted: no text description, the model works from the surrounding audio alone. The blue bar marks the part the model regenerated; the grey part is the original audio it had to stay consistent with. Each row plays the true clip next to the model's version.

Which stem actually changes. The stem model carries four streams — vocals, bass, drums and other — but in the runs below only the other stem is regenerated inside the edit window. Vocals, bass and drums are carried through from the original clip unchanged. What you are hearing is therefore the edit mechanism operating on a single stream, not a full four-stem rewrite.

Reading the modes. Scratch writes the whole clip with nothing to go on. Continuation is the ordinary causal behaviour: keep the opening, invent the rest. Interior is the contribution — the model rewrites a middle span and has to land back on the original audio that follows it. Prefix-less is the mirror image: the ending is fixed and the model has to invent a beginning that leads into it, something a purely causal model cannot do at all.

Keeping the voice

The aim here is to hold the voice fixed and rewrite what plays underneath it. The same caveat applies as above: the stream that actually gets regenerated is the other stem, so the vocal line survives intact while the accompaniment changes on that one stream rather than across the whole backing.
